\abstract{Almost every modern tokenizer treats whitespace as a hard delimiter:
no token may span a word boundary, however frequent the collocation.
The LiB (Less is Better) tokenizer crosses that border from first principles, learning
sub-word and supra-word units jointly under a single Minimum Description
Length (MDL) objective.
We present a production HuggingFace-compatible implementation of LiB and show
that what makes the MDL objective work in practice is a
cognitively inspired memorise-and-forget loop combining
an active route (reward re-ranking) and a passive route (decay of unused units).
With both routes operating, the vocabulary size is no longer a hyperparameter
but an emergent equilibrium: across seven typologically diverse languages,
LiB settles at 13.6k--28.4k units under a 50k cap.
Ablating passive forgetting inflates the lexicon by 38--88\% and worsens
description length by 2--10\% in every language, while leaving trigram
bits-per-character unchanged.%: the retained units are dead weight that only
%MDL prices.
%Budget-matched controls show that LiB's favourable description length stems
%from its freedom to choose its own vocabulary size; 
On predictive metrics,
%which are size-robust, 
LiB outperforms vocabulary size-matched BPE on trigram
bits-per-character in six of seven languages and size-matched SuperBPE, the
leading frequency-based supra-word tokenizer, in all seven. On a
tokenization-fair neural bits-per-byte evaluation it matches 50k BPE with
vocabularies of 27--57\% of that size, but every baseline retrained at LiB's
size outperforms it, by 2.8--10.2\%.
Supra-word tokens help LiB on every metric in every space-delimited language, and the
fraction it discovers doubles as a typological diagnostic, tracking
script and word-internal morphological complexity.
We release the Rust implementation, trained tokenizers, and all evaluation
code.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================

Almost every modern tokenizer refuses to infer a token above the word level.
Byte-Pair Encoding \citep[BPE;][]{sennrich-etal-2016-neural}, WordPiece
\citep{schuster2012japanese,wu2016googles}, and the unigram language model
\citep{kudo-2018-subword,kudo-richardson-2018-sentencepiece} all learn units
strictly within word-like chunks.
Whitespace is a hard border: no matter how often \emph{of the} or
\emph{New York} recurs, these methods will never store it as a single token.
%The constraint bites in practice, not merely in principle: across the three
%50k baseline vocabularies we train in Section~\ref{sec:setup}, not one of
%150{,}000 units contains an internal space.

The constraint is not ignored in the literature. Where it is stated, however, it is justified
on computational rather than linguistic grounds.
\citet{sennrich-etal-2016-neural} remark: ``for efficiency,
we do not consider pairs that cross word boundaries. The algorithm can thus be
run on the dictionary extracted from a text, with each word being weighted by
its frequency.'' Confining merges to within words is what allows BPE to run
over a vocabulary of types rather than over running text, which is a large
constant-factor saving. \citet{kudo-2018-subword} adopts the restriction by
citation, noting only that, similar to \citet{sennrich-etal-2016-neural}, he
does not consider subwords that cross word boundaries. The WordPiece
description in \citet{wu2016googles} does not raise the question at all: words
are broken into so-called wordpieces, and the boundary symbol exists so that the
original sequence can be recovered.

These papers do argue carefully for
breaking the word downwards. \citet{sennrich-etal-2016-neural} motivate
subword units by the open-vocabulary problem and by languages with productive
word formation, where translation ``require[s] mechanisms that go below the
word level'', and \citet{schuster2012japanese} developed WordPiece to segment
Japanese and Korean, languages that do not mark word boundaries at all. No
comparable argument is offered against building upwards. The downward move was
defended as a claim about language; the upward prohibition was adopted as an
implementation convenience. %, and the two were never weighed on the same terms.

Introducing the SentencePiece tokenizer, \citet{kudo-richardson-2018-sentencepiece} come closest to reopening the question, and for reasons close to ours. They drop the pre-tokenization requirement because ``we can not always assume that pre-tokenization is
available, especially for non-segmented languages'' and because it obstructs
lossless tokenization, and they report that pre-tokenization can even hurt
translation quality into Japanese. SentencePiece accordingly treats whitespace
as an ordinary symbol rather than as a boundary. However, its pieces still do not
span a space. %, so the border outlived the reform that might have removed it.

The convention was inherited from pipelines built for English, a language
of which the short, mostly monomorphemic, space-delimited words make the word a
convenient atom.
\citet{blasi2022overreliance} document how systematically the idiosyncrasies
of English have been mistaken for language-general facts across the cognitive
sciences, skewing research priorities towards the features English happens to
have and encouraging researchers to read findings from English speakers as
universal.
Arguably, tokenization is a consequential instance of that skew.
Here, the assumption is compiled into the infrastructure: every downstream model inherits the
whitespace border from the tokenizer it was trained with, whatever language it
is asked to process.
For the majority of the world's languages, whose compressible structure sits
below the word (because of agglutination, compounding, or templatic morphology) or above it
(collocation), that border is arguably in the wrong place.

Recent work has begun to question the border.
SuperBPE \citep{liu2025superbpe} lets BPE merge across whitespace in a second
training stage, reporting downstream gains with less LLM inference
compute.
From a different tradition, the LiB tokenizer
\citep{yang2020less,yang2024rethinking} derives sub- and supra-word units
jointly from a single Minimum Description Length (MDL) objective
\citep{rissanen1978modeling}. \citet{yang2022eyefixations} show that its
segmentations predict human eye fixations during reading better than
word-based ones, which they take as behavioural evidence that multi-word units are
psycholinguistically plausible \citep{wray2002formulaic,arnon2021starting}.

Here, we study what it takes to make such an MDL tokenizer work at
production scale, across typologically diverse languages, and under fair
evaluation.
We find that forgetting is what makes an MDL tokenizer effective.
An MDL lexicon needs to be able to shed units as readily as it admits them, in order to avoid the long-term storage of candidates that never repay their storage cost.
Following \citet{yang2020less}, our implementation combines two forgetting
routes that mirror the active and passive forgetting observed in human
memory: an active route that demotes, and eventually deletes, units
that lose head-to-head segmentation comparisons, and a passive route
that places rarely used units on probation and lets them expire.
With both routes operating, the vocabulary size ceases to be a hyperparameter:
admission and forgetting reach a language-specific equilibrium.
%well below the
%nominal budget, and the units that survive have earned their place.

We evaluate on seven typologically diverse languages with a battery of
metrics: description length (DL), Kneser--Ney $n$-gram bits-per-character
(BPC), a tokenization-fair neural bits-per-byte (BPB) evaluation, sequence
fertility, and encoding speed. Controls keep the comparison fair,
including a passive-forgetting ablation and budget-matched baselines
trained at LiB's self-organized vocabulary sizes. The contributions offered by this study are the following:

\begin{itemize}
  \item We give an explicit algorithmic account of LiB's two-route
        memorise-and-forget training loop. We show that passive forgetting is
        functional: ablating the passive route inflates the lexicon by
        38--88\% and worsens description length (DL) by 2--10\% in all seven languages, while
        trigram BPC is unaffected. %The ablated lexicon carries dead weight
        %that only a description-length lens reveals.
  \item We show that the vocabulary size self-regulates (13.6k--28.4k under a
        50k cap, tracking the language).% and we give an account of
        %what that buys: 
        Budget-matched baselines reveal that LiB's DL
        advantage over standard 50k-vocabulary baselines is a
        vocabulary-size effect. LiB beats size-matched BPE
        on trigram BPC in six of seven languages and size-matched SuperBPE
        in all seven.
  \item We provide a tokenization-fair neural evaluation. Under
        bits-per-byte with a small Transformer LM, LiB is level with 50k BPE
        in all seven languages, but baselines retrained at LiB's vocabulary
        size, SuperBPE included, outperform it in every language. Supra-word
        tokens help LiB on every metric in every space-delimited language.
  \item We introduce the supra-word ratio of the induced vocabulary as a
        data-driven typological diagnostic, tracking script and the complexity
        of word-internal morphology.
  \item We release LiB as a production HuggingFace-compatible Rust
        implementation with trained tokenizers and all evaluation code.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% ============================================================

\paragraph{Subword tokenization.}
BPE \citep{sennrich-etal-2016-neural}, WordPiece \citep{schuster2012japanese},
and Unigram LM \citep{kudo-2018-subword} are the dominant subword tokenization
methods; SentencePiece \citep{kudo-richardson-2018-sentencepiece} provides
language-agnostic implementations of both.
\citet{bostrom2020bpe} show that BPE's greedy merge order recovers
morphologically less coherent units than Unigram LM, with measurable downstream
effects.
\citet{rust2021howgood} demonstrate that these methods systematically
under-represent morphologically rich languages in multilingual models;
\citet{toraman2023impact} confirm this for Turkish specifically, where a
morphology-aware tokenizer outperforms BPE on six downstream tasks.
\citet{mielke2021words} survey the full trajectory from word-level to
character-level to subword models and conclude that no single tokenization
approach is optimal across all languages.

\paragraph{Tokenization fairness and the cost of English bias.}
\citet{blasi2022overreliance} document cognitive science's over-reliance on
English; the same critique applies to tokenization's design defaults, from
whitespace pre-tokenization to the 32k--50k budget convention.
\citet{petrov2023unfairness} show that the same text translated into different
languages can differ in token count by up to 15$\times$ under standard
multilingual tokenizers.
\citet{ahia2023languages} document the economic consequence: speakers of
morphologically rich and non-Latin-script languages pay more API tokens per
task while receiving worse results.
\citet{gutierrez-vasques2023looking} analyse BPE compression across 47
languages and show that merge patterns cluster by typological distance from
English.
\citet{zouhar2023tokenization} frame tokenization as an information channel and
show that Rényi entropy of a tokenization predicts downstream quality,
providing an information-theoretic basis for MDL-based tokenizers.

\paragraph{MDL, segmentation, and forgetting.}
The connection between segmentation and MDL goes back to
\citet{brent1999efficient}.
\citet{yang2020less} formalised the LiB objective and built forgetting into
the model as a mechanism; \citet{yang2024rethinking} argued for
an integrated sub- and supra-word vocabulary grounded in the Principle of
Least Effort, and \citet{yang2022eyefixations} showed that LiB segmentations
predict human eye fixations during reading.
The present work contributes the production-scale implementation, the
explicit active/passive algorithmic account, and the ablation evidence that
the forgetting mechanism, rather than the objective alone, is what makes
the approach competitive.

\paragraph{Supra-word tokens.}
Multi-word expressions have a long history in NLP
\citep{sag2002multiword,constant2017multiword}.
\citet{gee2023multiword} enrich a pretrained tokenizer's vocabulary with
frequent MWEs, achieving $\sim$50\% sequence compression with negligible
performance loss.
Most closely related is SuperBPE \citep{liu2025superbpe}, which extends BPE
merges across whitespace and gains up to $+4\%$ across 30 downstream tasks
with 27\% less inference compute.
LiB differs in mechanism and consequence: units are admitted by description
length rather than frequency, retained only while they earn their keep, and
the vocabulary size is discovered rather than imposed, yielding better
trigram BPC than SuperBPE at equal budgets, at the cost of SuperBPE's
superior sequence compression.
The psycholinguistic case for multi-word lexical units is made by
\citet{wray2002formulaic}, \citet{siyanova2011collocation},
\citet{christiansen2017more}, and \citet{arnon2021starting}.

\section{The LiB Tokenizer}
\label{sec:lib}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Objective}

The LiB objective \citep{yang2020less} is to find a token vocabulary $V$ and a
segmentation function $f$ such that the Description Length of the corpus $C$ is
minimised:

\begin{align}
  \mathrm{DL}(V, C) &= \mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{lex}(V) + \mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{corp}(C, V) \\
  \mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{lex}(V) &= -\sum_{s} \mathrm{Freq}(s) \log_2 P(s) \\
  \mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{corp}(C, V) &= -\sum_{u \in f(C)} \mathrm{Freq}(u) \log_2 P(u)
\end{align}

where $s$ ranges over the atomic symbols (characters) that make up the
vocabulary and $u$ ranges over the token types in the encoded corpus $C$.
Minimising the sum encourages both a compact, regular vocabulary
(a small $\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{lex}$) and a highly compressive encoding
(a small $\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{corp}$).

\subsection{Candidates Without a Whitespace Border}

Training begins with a seed vocabulary of all observed characters.
Candidate tokens are concatenations of adjacent chunks in the current
segmentation of the training text, spanning whatever
boundaries the text presents. There is no prior language-specific assumption about word or
morpheme boundaries. For different languages, different types of useful candidates may emerge:

\begin{itemize}
  \item In English, beneficial candidates often span spaces, yielding
        supra-word tokens such as \texttt{"\_of\_the"} or
        \texttt{"\_New\_York"}.
  \item In German, candidates may remain within long compounds, yielding
        tokens corresponding to roots or affixes.
  \item In Finnish or Turkish, candidates may systematically recover
        morpheme-length units.
  \item In Mandarin Chinese, written in an unspaced logographic script,
        candidates operate over character sequences and discover frequent
        multi-character words.
\end{itemize}
Whether a candidate survives in the lexicon is decided by the
memorise-and-forget dynamics described next.

\subsection{Memorising and Forgetting}
\label{sec:forgetting}

LiB follows the Principle of Least Effort by keeping the lexicon $L$ small: it
must forget units as readily as it memorises them, or the lexicon grows
without bound and $\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{lex}$ is never controlled.
\citet{yang2020less} accordingly build forgetting into the model as a
%first-class 
mechanism with two routes: an active route that demotes
units that proved unhelpful, and a passive route that lets unused units decay,
mirroring the active and passive forgetting observed in human memory.
%We make both routes explicit here, as together they are central to how LiB
%adapts its vocabulary size to a corpus.

The lexicon $L$ is a priority-ordered list: each unit $u$ has an ordinal
position $\Theta(u)$, with $\Theta=0$ at the head. Segmentation itself never
consults the ranking (rather, it performs a greedy longest-match with a one-chunk
lookahead), so $\Theta$ functions purely as a memory: it encodes how strongly a unit is
currently held, and it helps decide which units are at risk of being forgotten.
Training proceeds over documents; each epoch draws one document of
$d$ sentences ($d=\texttt{doc\_size}$). For each document, the following steps are taken:

\begin{description}
  \item[Read \& memorise.] Each sentence is segmented with the current $L$;
    with probability $\alpha$ (\texttt{memory\_in}) the concatenation of two
    adjacent chunks is proposed as a new candidate unit; an unknown character
    sequence of length ${\le}2$ may itself be a candidate. A candidate
    sighted twice within the same document is admitted, provided
    $|L|$ is below the cap $|V|$. New units enter at the tail, the lowest
    rank, and must earn their way up.
  \item[Evaluate (active forgetting).] At each position the segmenter also
    considers the second-longest match $c_2$ against the longest $c_1$; both
    branches are expanded greedily until they reconverge, and $c_1$ is
    judged bad if its branch needs more unknown symbols, or 
    %(at equal unknowns) 
    more chunks, or 
    %(at equal both) 
    a higher ordinal sum
    \citep[\S 2.3.1]{yang2020less}. A good $c_1$ is promoted headward,
    $\Theta\leftarrow\Theta-(\lfloor\Theta\Delta\rfloor+1)$ with
    $\Delta=\texttt{update\_rate}$; a bad $c_1$ is demoted tailward by the
    same step. A unit demoted past the tail ($\Theta\ge|L|$) is
    deleted outright, while the longest match of its shortened form is
    promoted in its place \citep[\S 2.3.2]{yang2020less}. Promotion is thus
    multiplicative: units that keep winning comparisons converge
    exponentially fast to the head of the vocabulary. %, where they are safe.
  \item[Probation \& expiry (passive forgetting).] Once per document, every
    unit on the probation watch has its remaining life decremented, and units
    whose life expires are forgotten. Then, the current tail, the bottom
    fraction $\omega$ (\texttt{memory\_out}) of $L$, is placed on probation with
    life $\lambda$ (\texttt{life}); seed units start on watch. Probation is
    cancelled the moment a unit is used in a segmentation without being judged
    bad, so
    only units that go $\lambda$ consecutive documents without proving
    themselves are lost.
\end{description}

Atomic units (single characters, byte-fallback tokens) and special tokens are
exempt from both deletion routes, guaranteeing lossless reconstruction.
The two routes play complementary roles. Active forgetting is
comparative: it punishes units that lose against an available alternative. Passive forgetting is absolute: it removes
units that are not used, after a grace period of $\lambda$
documents. A unit that stops being useful is first
demoted out of the safe head region, then drifts into the watched tail, and
finally expires.

The vocabulary size is not imposed by the cap $|V|$: it is an
emergent equilibrium between admission and the two forgetting routes, governed
by $\omega$.

%Section~\ref{sec:multilingual}: rather than an accident of one language reaching
%a wall, saturation is the general behaviour of the model once forgetting is
%strong enough to balance admission.

\subsection{Space Convention}

Spaces need to be properly reconstructed in detokenization. Following the Metaspace convention used by BPE and SentencePiece, a leading
space is prepended to each token to mark word-initial position
(e.g.\ \texttt{"\_cat"}).
For supra-word tokens, the space is infixed to mark internal word boundaries
(e.g.\ \texttt{"\_the\_cat"}).
This convention ensures lossless reconstruction of the original text and is
consistent with standard tokenizer behaviour.
For languages written without spaces (Chinese), text is fed without any space
insertion; LiB then operates directly on character sequences.

\subsection{Implementation}

We implement LiB as a custom \texttt{Model} in the HuggingFace
\texttt{tokenizers} Rust
library.\footnote{Fork: \url{https://github.com/antalvdb/tokenizers}, branch
\texttt{lib-model}.}
A Python wrapper (\texttt{LiBTokenizerFast}) exposes the HuggingFace
\texttt{PreTrainedTokenizerFast} interface.
The pre-trained English tokenizer is available at
\url{https://huggingface.co/antalvdb/lib-tokenizer}.

\subsection{Data}

For each language we sample 100,000 sentences for training and 10,000
sentences for evaluation from Wikipedia via the HuggingFace \texttt{datasets}
library \citep{lhoest2021datasets}.
Wikipedia provides topically consistent, high-quality text across all seven
languages with broad genre coverage.
Chinese text is used without word-boundary segmentation, allowing LiB and the
baselines to operate directly on character sequences.

\subsection{Tokenizers}

We compare the following tokenizers, all with a vocabulary budget of 50k:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{BPE}: HuggingFace \texttt{tokenizers} with Metaspace
        pre-tokenizer.
  \item \textbf{WordPiece}: HuggingFace \texttt{tokenizers} with whitespace
        pre-tokenizer.
  \item \textbf{SP-Unigram}: SentencePiece Unigram
        (\texttt{character\_coverage=1.0}, so that every character seen in
        training stays in the inventory and the model is not credited for
        discarding rare characters).
  \item \textbf{SuperBPE}: BPE with a second, cross-whitespace merge stage
        \citep{liu2025superbpe}.%, the leading frequency-based supra-word
        %tokenizer.
  \item \textbf{LiB}: our implementation, evaluated with and without
        supra-word lookup, plus the ablations and controls below.
  \item \textbf{Symbol}: a baseline tokenizer that segments every single character.
\end{itemize}

All tokenizers are trained on the per-language training set.
All tokenizers receive Chinese text without word segmentation.

\paragraph{LiB configuration.}
Hyperparameters were selected by random search on held-out validation BPC for
English, Finnish, Turkish, and Chinese (Section~\ref{sec:hparam}).
The space-delimited languages share a consensus configuration
($\alpha{=}0.25$, $\Delta{=}0.4$, $\omega{=}0.005$, $\lambda{=}50$,
$d{=}100$, $\texttt{max\_len}{=}16$, 5{,}000 document-epochs); Chinese uses
its own searched configuration ($\omega{=}0.01$, $\lambda{=}10$, $d{=}50$,
$\texttt{max\_len}{=}20$).

\paragraph{Controls.}
Three additional LiB variants isolate the mechanisms at work:
LiB (no forget) ablates passive forgetting ($\omega{=}0$, everything
else unchanged); LiB (cap) and LiB (cap2) raise the admission
rate ($\alpha{=}0.5$; cap2 additionally weakens decay to $\omega{=}0.001$) so
that the English lexicon approaches (46.7k) and reaches (50.0k) the budget.
%giving a budget ladder from the self-regulated size to the cap.
Finally, budget-matched baselines retrain BPE, WordPiece, and
SP-Unigram at each language's emergent LiB vocabulary size, separating
vocabulary-size effects from tokenizer-quality effects.

\subsection{Metrics}

\paragraph{Description Length.}
$\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{lex}$, $\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{corp}$, and
$\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{total}$ in kilobits, following \citet{yang2020less}.
DL is LiB's own training objective; we therefore treat it as a diagnostic of
whether the mechanism achieves what it optimises, and pair it with the
independent metrics below.

\paragraph{Bits per Character (BPC).}
Kneser--Ney interpolated bigram and trigram language models
\citep{kneser-ney-1995}, trained with KenLM \citep{heafield2011kenlm} on the
tokenised training corpus and evaluated on the tokenised test corpus.
BPC = total surprisal / total characters in raw test text; the
character normalisation makes scores comparable across tokenizations.

\paragraph{Neural bits per byte (BPB).}
$n$-gram estimators penalise rare token types through data sparsity, which
systematically disadvantages any tokenizer with a long tail of supra-word
types.
As a tokenization-fair complement we train one small decoder-only Transformer LM per
tokenizer (4 layers, 4 heads, $d{=}256$, context 256; identical architecture,
training schedule, and data for every tokenizer) and report cross-entropy
over the test stream normalised by its raw UTF-8 byte count.

\paragraph{Fertility.}
Tokens per byte on the test set, the determinant of sequence length and therefore an estimator of inference cost \citep{liu2025superbpe}.

\paragraph{Supra-word token ratio.}
The proportion of vocabulary entries containing an internal space
(a leading Metaspace marker does not count), a proxy for the degree to which
LiB invests its vocabulary above rather than below the word.

\paragraph{Encoding throughput.}
Characters per second, measured on an otherwise idle machine over 3
repetitions on 1,000 test lines after a warm-up pass.

Two effects are visible.
%First, dead weight, the amount of units never used, falls 
%monotonically with $\omega$: 
First, the never-used supra-word

least-used units and leaves more room for other units to earn their place. The lexicon
does not merely shrink; its composition improves continuously.
Second, the effect of $\omega$ saturates. Most of the reduction is already
achieved by $\omega=5\times10^{-3}$; raising it fourfold to
$\omega=2\times10^{-2}$ removes only a further 241 units. The lexicon
does not ever fill its budget at this admission rate either: with the passive route switched

hyperparameter. Standard tokenizers fill the $|V|$ they are given; LiB, with
forgetting active, discovers the vocabulary size at which no further unit repays
its storage cost, and $\omega$ tunes how aggressively it does so.

The supra-word
ratio likewise becomes a controllable, emergent quantity rather than a fixed
property, which we relate to typology in Section~\ref{sec:multilingual}.

Three observations stand out.
First, most of these units are not multi-word expressions in the lexicographic
sense. Rather than whole-word bigrams, LiB's most productive units

following function word: English \texttt{s of}, German \texttt{en und},

regularity lies in the recurring join between a morphological ending
and a following functor.

morphology inversely: the richer the word-internal morphology, the more of the
budget is already consumed below the word.

not of Finnish or Turkish generally, and they are a concrete instance of the
register limitation noted in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity}
\label{sec:hparam}

We ran random search (10 trials per language) over all five training
hyperparameters plus document size and the number of document-epochs, on
English, Finnish, Turkish, and Chinese, scoring held-out validation 3-gram BPC
at the 50k cap.
The three space-delimited languages agree on a consensus configuration
($d{=}100$, $\texttt{max\_len}{=}16$, $\lambda{=}50$, $\omega{=}0.005$,
$\Delta{=}0.4$), which we adopt for all space-delimited languages.
The forgetting ratio $\omega$ sets where the admission--forgetting equilibrium
falls, within the range the admission rate allows
(Section~\ref{sec:forgetting-results});
$\alpha$ trades vocabulary size against a marginal BPC gain (the
BPC-best $\alpha{=}0.5$ brings English to the cap for $\approx$0.01 bits over
the self-regulating $\alpha{=}0.25$, which we prefer for its emergent size
and faster training).
Remaining hyperparameters affect validation BPC by under 0.03 bits across
the searched ranges.

LiB's supra-word tokens bring its fertility from that of the no-supra
variant, which produces 2.0--2.4 times as many tokens in the
space-delimited languages, into the range of the
subword baselines, but not below it: MDL admission optimises description length,
not sequence length.
SuperBPE appears to be the choice for raw sequence
compression; LiB offers a self-sizing vocabulary with the neural BPB of 50k
BPE.


%% ============================================================
\section{A Joint Multilingual Vocabulary}
\label{sec:joint}
%% ============================================================

Here, we train a single tokenizer on the data of all seven languages and test
it on each language separately.

\paragraph{Setup.}
The joint training set concatenates the seven 100,000-sentence training sets.
BPE, WordPiece, SP-Unigram, and SuperBPE are trained on it with the 50k
budget, and BPE, WordPiece, and SP-Unigram are trained again at LiB's emergent
joint size. LiB uses the consensus configuration for all seven languages,
Chinese included, with 35{,}000 document-epochs (seven times the monolingual
schedule) and a cap of 350k, which it does not reach. Each LiB document holds
100 sentences of a single language. A candidate is admitted when it occurs
twice within one document, and documents that mix languages would dilute that
evidence. Every tokenizer is tested on each language's own test set. The
trigram and neural LMs are trained once per tokenizer on the joint training
stream and scored per language. The neural LMs keep the 5{,}000-step schedule
of Section~\ref{sec:bpb}, which gives each language about a seventh of its
monolingual exposure, so the joint BPB figures are comparable with each other
but not with Table~\ref{tab:bpb}.

\paragraph{Vocabulary.}
The joint LiB vocabulary settles at 116,322 units, which is 33\% of the cap
and 69\% of the 167,504 units of the seven monolingual vocabularies combined
(Table~\ref{tab:supra}). Of these units, 16.8\% are supra-word.
Table~\ref{tab:joint_alloc} breaks the vocabulary down by the units each
language's test set uses. Chinese and Arabic, the two languages not written
in Latin script, use the largest numbers of units that no other language
uses. The supra-word share of the units each language uses keeps the
typological split of Section~\ref{sec:multilingual}: Spanish, Arabic, and
English use the most supra-word units (17--19\%), German, Turkish, and
Finnish fewer (9--13\%), and Chinese almost none (1.6\%).

\paragraph{Cross-testing.}
Sharing the vocabulary leaves LiB's segmentation of each language almost
unchanged. On the six space-delimited languages, the joint tokenizer's
fertility lies within 2.4\% of the monolingual LiB tokenizer's. On Chinese it
is 5.2\% lower; the joint run applies the consensus configuration, whose
passive forgetting is weaker than that of the Chinese configuration
($\omega{=}0.005$ and $\lambda{=}50$, against $0.01$ and $10$). The baselines
trained at the same joint size need 4--21\% more tokens than their
monolingual counterparts, and Turkish is the most affected language for
each of them (BPE, WordPiece, and SP-Unigram). This comparison favours LiB,
since each baseline goes from 50k units per language (350k in total) to 116k
shared units, while LiB goes from 168k in total to 116k. It does show that
the joint equilibrium preserves each language's monolingual segmentation
without a per-language budget.

1.7\% behind elsewhere (Finnish). A larger vocabulary raises trigram BPC in
the joint setting: BPE at LiB's size is worse than 50k BPE in every language
except Finnish, which reflects the estimator sparsity discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:bpb}, and LiB carries the same 116k vocabulary. On Chinese,
LiB without supra-word lookup has the lowest trigram BPC of all tokenizers.

ahead of SuperBPE in five languages, level on Spanish, and behind on Chinese.

to 0.21 (Turkish), and without it LiB needs 2.0--2.3 times as many tokens in
the space-delimited languages.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% ============================================================

Studying the effect of active and passive forgetting, we observed that with passive forgetting removed
and everything else held fixed, the lexicon inflates by 38--88\%, description
length worsens by 2--10\% in every language, and trigram BPC does not move.
Prediction-based metrics are insensitive to unproductive tokens in the vocabulary, since a unit that
rarely fires costs a smoothed LM almost nothing. In other words, a vocabulary can look
healthy
under BPC while carrying thousands of unproductive types.
The MDL lens is the one metric that prices this storage.
The $\omega$-sweep (Table~\ref{tab:omega}) shows the same mechanism from a
different angle: as forgetting strengthens, both the emergent lexicon size and
its never-used fraction fall monotonically, until the effect saturates and
further forgetting has almost nothing left to remove.

Against the standard fixed-budget baselines, LiB attains the best description
length in five of seven languages; against baselines granted LiB's own
vocabulary size, it loses in all seven.
Both observations are informative.
The first tells that the self-regulation is doing productive work,
most dramatically on Finnish
($-19.5\%$).
The second observation says that LiB's per-budget encoding is not better than the baselines',
and we do not claim it is.
What we do claim is that LiB is the only system in the
comparison for which vocabulary size is a result, discovered per
language by the admission--forgetting equilibrium, rather than a
hyperparameter.

Also, supra-word tokens help LiB on every metric in
every spaced language: DL, 2-gram and 3-gram BPC, neural BPB, fertility, and
even throughput.
This contrasts with SuperBPE, of which the frequency-selected, never-forgotten
supra-words win on fertility but lose BPC to plain BPE in all seven
languages.
Together the results of LiB and SuperBPE suggest that the supra-word level itself is not the
problem. How units are admitted, and above all whether unproductive units are
removed, determines whether crossing the whitespace border is useful.

Among the tested space-delimited languages, the supra-word ratio separates
the agglutinative and compounding languages (Finnish 14.7\%, German 17.5\%,
Turkish 19.1\%), which invest least above the word, from the analytic,
Romance, and templatic languages (English 25.7\%, Spanish 29.6\%, Arabic
30.5\%), which invest most.
The ratio operationalises where a language keeps its compressible
redundancy, discovered by a single objective with no language-specific
machinery.

Finally, a note on the label ``supra-word'', which in itself encodes an English bias. Supra-word tokens are ``supra'' only relative to the English word, which is an unusually short and
monomorphemic unit by cross-linguistic standards.
What LiB discovers at the equivalent compression point in Finnish is
morpheme-length, sub-word in English terms, but linguistically fundamental
in Finnish.
The MDL objective makes no such distinction, which is precisely its value
for equitable multilingual tokenization
\citep{petrov2023unfairness,ahia2023languages}.

\section{Limitations}

Description length is LiB's own training objective; we therefore lean on the independent
metrics (BPC, BPB, fertility) for cross-tokenizer claims, and on the
ablation, not the baseline comparison, for the mechanism claim.
Our neural evaluation uses a small LM; whether the 5.8--10.2\% BPB gap to the
best baseline at LiB's vocabulary size persists, shrinks, or reverses at scale, and how these
differences translate to downstream task quality, remains open. SuperBPE's
downstream gains \citep{liu2025superbpe} suggest that intrinsic metrics
undersell supra-word tokenizers.

The BPB figures are also sensitive to the training budget. Every tokenizer is
trained for the same 5{,}000 steps, so the comparison is internally
consistent, but extending that budget to 8{,}000 steps moved a single
tokenizer's score by $4.6\%$, which exceeds most of the gaps we report. The
BPB column should therefore be read as a ranking at a fixed budget rather
than as a set of stable absolute margins. Vocabulary size has a similar
effect. Monolingually, BPE, WordPiece, and SuperBPE score 1--4\% better at
LiB's smaller sizes than at 50k, while SP-Unigram does not improve; in the
joint setting, WordPiece is worse at LiB's larger size and SP-Unigram
better.

Wikipedia may underrepresent colloquial registers; Chinese preprocessing
(no word segmentation) makes its results a script-level, not
morphology-level, comparison; and the cap-filling variants change the
admission rate alongside decay, so the budget ladder is indicative rather
than a single-knob sweep above 46.7k.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ============================================================

We set out to build a production-grade tokenizer that learns sub- and
supra-word units jointly under a Minimum Description Length objective, and
found that the decisive component is the memory management. A memorise-and-forget loop with two cognitively grounded routes, active
demotion of units that lose segmentation comparisons and passive expiry of
units that stop being used, turns the vocabulary size into a language-specific
emergent equilibrium and keeps the lexicon free of dead weight that
prediction-based metrics cannot measure.
Ablating passive forgetting inflates the lexicon by up to 88\% and worsens
description length in all seven languages while leaving trigram BPC
untouched.
%: forgetting is load-bearing, and description length is the only
%standard metric that notices.

Evaluated fairly, with budget-matched controls and a tokenization-fair
neural metric, LiB does not beat the best subword tokenizers.
It matches 50k BPE on neural BPB with vocabularies of 27--57\% of that
size, although every baseline retrained at LiB's size is better. It beats the
frequency-based supra-word alternative on trigram BPC in every language, at
50k and at LiB's size. It
discovers where each language keeps its compressible redundancy, and sizes
its own vocabulary, removing a hyperparameter of which the default values are an English-biased convention.
Trained jointly on all seven languages, it settles at 69\% of the size of
its seven monolingual vocabularies combined and segments each language
within 2.4\% of the fertility of its monolingual tokenizer (Chinese
excepted, where it needs 5\% fewer tokens).

Supra-word tokens, properly curated by forgetting, help on every metric in
every spaced language; the supra-word ratio itself can be taken as a typological
diagnostic, inversely
tracking word-internal morphological complexity among space-delimited languages.

We release the Rust implementation, trained tokenizers, and evaluation
suite. The clear next step is downstream evaluation at scale, where
SuperBPE's results suggest supra-word tokenizers are undersold by intrinsic
metrics.
