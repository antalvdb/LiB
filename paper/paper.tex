\begin{abstract}
Tokenization — the segmentation of text into discrete units — lies at the heart of
virtually every natural language processing system.
Yet the dominant tokenization methods (BPE, WordPiece, SentencePiece Unigram) were
developed and benchmarked almost exclusively on English, a typologically unusual
language with short, analytic, space-delimited words.
Following the broader critique of English bias in cognitive science
\citep{blasi2022overreliance}, we ask whether this bias is baked into the design
assumptions of standard tokenizers, and whether a language-agnostic alternative
can do better.

We present \textbf{LiB} (Less is Better), a tokenizer grounded in the Minimum
Description Length (MDL) principle: it learns the lexicon that jointly minimises
the number of distinct token types and the total number of tokens needed to encode
a corpus, making no language-specific assumptions.
A distinctive feature of LiB is its discovery of \emph{supra-word} tokens —
multi-word units encoding frequent collocations — as well as \emph{sub-word} tokens
for morphologically rich text, both emerging naturally from the MDL objective.
Crucially, \citet{yang2022eyefixations} show that LiB's segmentations predict
human eye fixations during reading better than word-based approaches, providing
psycholinguistic evidence that supra-word tokens reflect cognitively real units
stored in the mental lexicon.

We compare LiB against BPE, WordPiece, and SentencePiece Unigram across seven
typologically diverse languages — English, German, Finnish, Turkish, Arabic,
Spanish, and Chinese — spanning analytic, fusional, agglutinative, templatic, and
logographic language types.
We evaluate using Description Length in kilobits and Bits-per-Character (BPC)
via Kneser-Ney $n$-gram language models.
Our results show that while LiB trails WordPiece in raw DL and BPC, the gap is
smallest for agglutinative languages (Finnish: $+13\%$ DL, Turkish: $+15\%$ DL)
and largest for logographic Chinese ($+43\%$), where the absence of spaces
prevents supra-word token formation.
Supra-word tokens adapt in number and character to the morphological profile of
each language, providing a natural typological fingerprint.

We release LiB as a production-ready HuggingFace-compatible tokenizer backed by
a Rust library, and provide all training and evaluation code.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================

Modern NLP systems begin with tokenization: the process of segmenting raw text
into discrete symbolic units that serve as the model's vocabulary.
The dominant tokenization methods — Byte-Pair Encoding \citep[BPE;][]{sennrich-etal-2016-neural},
WordPiece \citep{schuster1999japanese,wu2016googles}, and SentencePiece Unigram
\citep{kudo-2018-subword,kudo-richardson-2018-sentencepiece} — were introduced
to handle the open vocabulary problem: any word, no matter how rare or morphologically
complex, can be represented as a sequence of subword units drawn from a finite
vocabulary.

Yet these methods carry an implicit English bias.
English has a number of typologically unusual properties: it is largely analytic
(grammatical relationships are expressed through word order and function words,
not inflection), it has limited compounding, most of its content words are short
and monomorphemic, and it uses spaces to delimit words.
By contrast, many of the world's languages differ substantially along all these
dimensions: Germanic languages such as German, Dutch, and Swedish form long
compounds freely; agglutinative languages such as Finnish and Turkish express
in a single word what English would express in an entire clause; languages
with templatic morphology such as Arabic build words through non-linear root-pattern
combinations; and logographic writing systems such as Chinese do not mark word
boundaries with spaces at all.

This situation parallels the broader critique of English bias in cognitive science
articulated by \citet{blasi2022overreliance}: cognitive scientists have
over-relied on English, warping research programs by over-emphasising features
present in English and over-generalising findings from English speakers to our
entire species.
We argue that the same critique applies to tokenization research.
The design choices behind BPE, WordPiece, and SentencePiece Unigram — what
constitutes a ``good'' token, what the right vocabulary size is, how spaces
relate to word boundaries — were motivated by and validated against English text.
These choices may actively harm tokenization quality for the majority of the
world's languages.

As an alternative, we propose and evaluate LiB (Less is Better), a tokenizer
grounded in the Minimum Description Length principle \citep{rissanen1978modeling}.
LiB makes no assumption about what a token should look like: it discovers
whatever units minimise the total cost of encoding a corpus, including the
codebook (lexicon) itself.
This language-agnostic objective means that LiB adapts its token inventory to
the distributional structure of each language: on English it discovers supra-word
tokens (multi-word collocations); on agglutinative languages it should discover
morpheme-length units; on compounding languages it should split compounds
at morpheme boundaries.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A typological critique of existing tokenization methods through the lens of
        English bias, supported by a systematic multi-language evaluation.
  \item A Rust implementation of LiB integrated into the HuggingFace
        \texttt{tokenizers} ecosystem, with a Python wrapper following the
        \texttt{PreTrainedTokenizerFast} interface.
  \item The first systematic comparison of LiB against BPE, WordPiece, and
        SentencePiece Unigram across seven typologically diverse languages,
        measuring Description Length and BPC.
  \item Evidence that LiB's MDL objective degrades most gracefully on
        agglutinative languages (Finnish: $+13\%$ DL gap vs.\ WordPiece;
        Turkish: $+15\%$), and that the space-based supra-word mechanism
        breaks down on logographic Chinese, which requires dedicated future treatment.
\end{itemize}

\section{English Bias in Tokenization}
\label{sec:bias}
%% ============================================================

\citet{blasi2022overreliance} document how cognitive science's over-reliance on
English speakers and English-language stimuli has biased the field in two ways:
by warping research programs (emphasising mechanisms salient in English) and by
overgeneralising English speakers' behaviour to all humans.
We argue that tokenization research is subject to the same biases.

\paragraph{Design assumptions tailored to English.}
BPE begins by splitting text on whitespace and treating each character as a seed
token, then merges the most frequent adjacent pair.
This procedure presupposes that spaces mark word boundaries — a feature of many
but not all writing systems — and that the most useful merges are within a single
word.
The resulting tokenization for English is close to the morphological analysis a
linguist would produce, because English words are short and mostly monomorphemic.

For a compounding language such as German, BPE must either (a) produce sub-word
tokens that do not correspond to morphemes (splitting arbitrary character sequences
within a compound), or (b) keep long compound words as single tokens, inflating
the vocabulary with words that appear only a few times in training.
Neither outcome is desirable, and neither reflects the principled morphological
analysis that would benefit downstream models.

For an agglutinative language such as Finnish, each word is a sequence of
morphemes, and a single wordform may correspond to dozens of inflectional and
derivational combinations.
BPE's greedy character-pair merging has no mechanism for discovering morpheme
boundaries; it will merge characters into tokens that span morpheme boundaries
arbitrarily.

For logographic Chinese, spaces are absent from the writing system, so BPE's
space-based pre-tokenization does not apply: the text is typically segmented
character by character (each character is roughly a morpheme), and BPE character
merges produce units of varying length without any morphological grounding.

\paragraph{Vocabulary size as an English-derived hyperparameter.}
The standard vocabulary sizes used in the literature (16k, 32k, 50k) were
established by practitioners working primarily with English.
For languages with much larger morphological paradigms, the optimal vocabulary
size may be different: a Finnish model may need far more subword types to cover
the space of inflected forms, while a Chinese model may do well with a smaller
character-level vocabulary.
LiB's MDL objective provides a principled stopping criterion that is sensitive
to the actual distributional structure of each language.

\paragraph{Evaluation on English.}
Benchmarks for tokenization quality — including the Description Length and BPC
metrics we use — have been applied almost exclusively to English corpora.
Whether these metrics behave similarly across languages, and which tokenizer
performs best on which language type, has not been systematically studied.


%% ============================================================
\section{The LiB Tokenizer}
\label{sec:lib}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Objective}

The LiB objective \citep{yang2020less} is to find a token vocabulary $V$ and a
segmentation function $f$ such that the Description Length of the corpus is minimised:

\begin{align}
  \mathrm{DL}(V, C) &= \mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{lex}(V) + \mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{corp}(C, V) \\
  \mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{lex}(V) &= -\sum_{s} \mathrm{Freq}(s) \log_2 P(s) \\
  \mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{corp}(C, V) &= -\sum_{u \in f(C)} \mathrm{Freq}(u) \log_2 P(u)
\end{align}

where $s$ ranges over the atomic symbols (characters) that make up the vocabulary
and $u$ ranges over the token types in the encoded corpus $C$.
Minimising the sum encourages both a compact, regular vocabulary
(small $\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{lex}$) and a highly compressive encoding
(small $\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{corp}$).

\subsection{Algorithm and Language Neutrality}

Training begins with a seed vocabulary of all observed characters.
At each epoch the algorithm proposes \emph{candidate} tokens by concatenating
adjacent token pairs in the current segmentation of the training corpus.
A candidate is accepted if adding it reduces the total DL.

Crucially, the candidates span whatever boundaries are present in the text.
There is no prior assumption about word or morpheme boundaries:
\begin{itemize}
  \item In English, the most beneficial merges often span spaces, yielding
        \emph{supra-word} tokens such as \texttt{"\_of\_the"} or \texttt{"\_New\_York"}.
  \item In German, the most beneficial merges may remain within long compounds,
        yielding tokens corresponding to roots or affixes.
  \item In Finnish or Turkish, merges may systematically recover morpheme boundaries.
  \item In Chinese (preprocessed without spaces), merges operate over character
        sequences and should discover frequent multi-character words.
\end{itemize}

The MDL objective adapts to each language's structure without requiring any
language-specific rules.

\subsection{Space Convention}

Following the Metaspace convention used by BPE and SentencePiece, a leading
space is \emph{prepended} to each token to mark word-initial position
(e.g.\ \texttt{"\_cat"}).
For supra-word tokens, the space is infixed to mark internal word boundaries
(e.g.\ \texttt{"\_the\_cat"}).
This convention ensures lossless reconstruction of the original text and is
consistent with standard tokenizer behaviour.
For languages without spaces (Chinese), text is fed without any space insertion;
LiB then operates directly on character sequences.

\subsection{Implementation}

We implement LiB as a custom \texttt{Model} in the HuggingFace \texttt{tokenizers}
Rust library.\footnote{Fork: \url{https://github.com/antalvdb/tokenizers}, branch \texttt{lib-model}.}
A Python wrapper (\texttt{LiBTokenizerFast}) exposes the HuggingFace
\texttt{PreTrainedTokenizerFast} interface.
The pre-trained English tokenizer is available at
\url{https://huggingface.co/antalvdb/lib-tokenizer}.

\subsection{Data}

For each language we sample 100,000 sentences for training and 10,000 sentences
for evaluation from Wikipedia via the HuggingFace \texttt{datasets} library
\citep{lhoest2021datasets}.
Wikipedia provides topically consistent, high-quality text across all seven
languages with broad genre coverage.
Chinese text is used without word-boundary segmentation, allowing LiB and the
baselines to operate directly on character sequences.

\subsection{Tokenizers}

We compare the following tokenizers at vocabulary sizes of 10k, 32k, and 50k:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{BPE}: HuggingFace \texttt{tokenizers} with Metaspace pre-tokenizer.
  \item \textbf{WordPiece}: HuggingFace \texttt{tokenizers} with whitespace pre-tokenizer.
  \item \textbf{SP-Unigram}: SentencePiece Unigram (\texttt{character\_coverage=0.9995}).
  \item \textbf{LiB}: our implementation, with and without supra-word lookup.
  \item \textbf{Symbol}: character baseline.
\end{itemize}

All tokenizers are trained on the per-language training set.
SentencePiece handles Chinese naturally through its byte-level fallback; BPE and
WordPiece are given character-split input for Chinese.

\subsection{Metrics}

\paragraph{Description Length.}
$\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{lex}$, $\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{corp}$, and $\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{total}$
in kilobits, following \citet{yang2020less}.

\paragraph{Bits per Character (BPC).}
Kneser-Ney interpolated bigram and trigram language models \citep{kneser-ney-1995}
trained on the tokenised training corpus (NLTK; \citealt{bird2009natural}),
evaluated on the tokenised test corpus.
BPC = total surprisal / total characters in raw test text.

\paragraph{Supra-word token ratio.}
The proportion of vocabulary entries containing an embedded space, as a
proxy for the degree to which LiB discovers multi-word units vs.\ sub-word units.
This ratio is expected to vary systematically across language types.

\paragraph{Encoding throughput.}
Characters per second and tokens per second, measured over 3 repetitions on
1,000 test lines after a warm-up pass.

while supra-word types are rarer and harder to predict.

\subsection{Supra-word Token Ratio Across Languages}

Table~\ref{tab:dl_multilingual} reveals a clear pattern in the supra-word ratio —
the fraction of LiB's vocabulary containing embedded spaces:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{English} (77\%), \textbf{Spanish} (76\%), \textbf{Arabic} (81\%):
        high ratios, dominated by multi-word collocations.
        Arabic's high ratio is surprising given its morphological complexity
        (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
  \item \textbf{German} (68\%), \textbf{Turkish} (69\%), \textbf{Finnish} (65\%):
        lower ratios, consistent with more within-word compression opportunity
        in compounding and agglutinative languages.
  \item \textbf{Chinese} (2\%): near-zero, as expected — without spaces in the
        input, LiB cannot form supra-word tokens and instead discovers
        multi-character sequences (average token length 5.2 characters).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Turkish Vocabulary Saturation}

A striking finding is that LiB's training on Turkish saturated at \textbf{28,951
vocabulary entries}, well short of the 50k target.
The MDL algorithm stopped accepting new candidates because no additional token —
supra-word or sub-word — reduced the total description length.
This reflects a fundamental property of agglutinative morphology: Turkish words
are built from a small set of roots and a productive but finite set of suffixes.
Once LiB has learned the frequent morpheme-length units, further merges do not pay
off.
By contrast, all other tokenizers force 50k entries regardless of whether each
additional entry compresses the data — they have no stopping criterion linked
to information gain.
Turkish LiB's DL\textsubscript{lex} (926 kb) is also much lower than all other
languages, because its compact 29k vocabulary is more regular.

\subsection{LiB Degrades Least on Agglutinative Languages}

Table~\ref{tab:dl_summary} shows the gap between LiB and the best baseline
(WordPiece) by language.
The gap is largest for Chinese (+43\%) and Arabic (+26\%),
moderate for analytic and fusional languages (English, German, Spanish: +22--23\%),
and \emph{smallest for the agglutinative languages} — Finnish (+13\%) and Turkish
(+15\%).
This is the central empirical result of the paper: LiB, trained with a
language-agnostic MDL objective, degrades most gracefully precisely on the
language types that are most different from English.
The English-biased tokenizers (BPE, WordPiece, SP-Unigram) retain their relative
advantage on English-like languages but offer no principled advantage on
morphologically rich ones.

The BPC pattern partially mirrors the DL pattern but with one notable reversal:
Turkish shows the \emph{smallest} BPC gap (+6.2\%) despite its high DL gap
(+15\%), suggesting that LiB's compact 29k vocabulary — though penalised by
absolute DL — produces a particularly regular token sequence on agglutinative
text.
Chinese remains the clear outlier: LiB's multi-character tokens, formed without
access to word-boundary cues, are both informationally expensive (DL: +43\%) and
poorly predictable (BPC: +16.3\%).

Comparing LiB with and without supra-word tokens reveals an $n$-gram order interaction:
at 2-gram, supra-words improve BPC in 6 of 7 languages (all except Chinese), because
supra-word tokens encode multi-word context within a single item, giving shorter 2-gram windows
more predictive power.
At 3-gram, this advantage reverses — full LiB has \emph{higher} BPC than
LiB\textsuperscript{--} in all seven languages.
With a 3-gram window, the model already captures multi-word context via token history,
so the additional context embedded in supra-words is redundant, while their lower
unigram frequency makes them harder to predict.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% ============================================================

\paragraph{LiB as an equitable tokenizer.}
The MDL objective is language-agnostic by construction: it discovers the token
inventory that minimises description length for whatever distributional structure
the input presents.
Unlike BPE, WordPiece, and SentencePiece — which assume that tokens are
subword units within space-delimited words — LiB can discover both sub-word and
supra-word tokens, adapting its vocabulary to the morphological and collocational
profile of each language.
The clearest evidence of this adaptation is the Turkish vocabulary saturation:
LiB stopped at 28,951 tokens because no further merge reduced description length,
while all baselines were forced to fill the full 50k budget regardless.

\paragraph{The Arabic anomaly.}
Arabic shows an unexpectedly high supra-word ratio (81\%), higher than English.
This may reflect the structure of Arabic Wikipedia: Modern Standard Arabic uses
many fixed multi-word phrases (idiomatic expressions, religious formulae,
bureaucratic collocations) that recur frequently enough to warrant supra-word
tokens.
Alternatively, Arabic's templatic morphology may produce surface forms that
co-occur so consistently that entire collocations compress better than individual
words.
The high supra-word ratio combined with high DL gap (+26\%) suggests LiB is
compressing Arabic at the wrong level of granularity — it finds multi-word
collocations instead of the morphological structure that would be more
linguistically appropriate.
This is a limitation worth investigating with morphologically annotated evaluation
data.

\paragraph{Chinese: a fundamentally different challenge.}
LiB performs worst on Chinese (DL gap +43\%), for a different reason than Arabic.
Without spaces, LiB cannot form supra-word tokens; instead it discovers
multi-character sequences, producing an average token length of 5.2 characters.
These long sequences capture some frequent multi-character words but miss the
character-level compositionality that makes Chinese logographic text distinctive.
BPE, which also operates character-by-character on Chinese, fares best (8,944 kb)
because its greedy merging of frequent character pairs naturally finds common
bigrams corresponding to frequent words.
The current LiB implementation's space-based supra-word mechanism is not designed
for logographic scripts, and Chinese requires dedicated treatment in future work.

\paragraph{Supra-word tokens as cognitively real units.}
On English, 78\% of LiB's 50k vocabulary consists of supra-word tokens:
multi-word units absent from all standard tokenizers.
The cognitive reality of these units is supported by \citet{yang2022eyefixations},
who show that LiB segmentations predict human eye fixations during reading better
than word-based segmentations — direct behavioural evidence that supra-word units
are stored in the mental lexicon.
This finding aligns with psycholinguistic work arguing that the mental lexicon
contains not just morphemes and words but also multi-word chunks
\citep{wray2002formulaic,siyanova2011collocation}.

A further observation concerns how LiB allocates its vocabulary budget as it
grows.
The number of supra-word types increases sharply with vocabulary size: 6,459
at 10k, 24,087 at 32k, and 38,731 at 50k (64\%, 75\%, and 78\% of the
vocabulary, respectively).
Rather than refining its inventory of single-word and sub-word tokens as the
budget expands — as BPE and WordPiece do — LiB consistently invests the
additional capacity in multi-word collocations.
This suggests that English text contains a long tail of moderately frequent
collocations, each individually compressive enough to warrant a token once
the vocabulary is large enough to accommodate it.
The implication for multilingual use is that the optimal vocabulary allocation
will look qualitatively different across language types: on agglutinative
languages the additional budget is more likely to go to morphologically complex
stems or suffix combinations, rather than to multi-word units.

Importantly, the label ``supra-word'' already encodes an English bias:
it is supra- relative to the English \emph{word}, a unit that is unusually short
and monomorphemic by cross-linguistic standards.
In agglutinative languages, what LiB discovers at the equivalent compression
point will look more like morpheme-length units — sub-word in English terms,
but cognitively and linguistically fundamental in their respective languages.
The MDL objective makes no such distinction, discovering whatever units best
compress the corpus without privileging the English word as the natural unit
of analysis.

\paragraph{The supra-word ratio as a typological diagnostic.}
We introduce the supra-word token ratio as a new, data-driven diagnostic of
how a tokenizer adapts to language structure.
A high ratio (as in English) indicates strong multi-word collocational structure
and limited within-word compression opportunity; a low ratio indicates the opposite.
We predict this ratio will correlate with standard typological measures of
morphological complexity (e.g.\ morpheme-per-word ratios), providing a
tokenization-based operationalisation of the analytic-to-synthetic continuum.

\paragraph{Implications for multilingual models.}
Most multilingual language models (mBERT, XLM-R, mT5) use a shared subword
vocabulary trained on many languages simultaneously.
The English bias in tokenization means that English words are typically represented
as single tokens, while words in morphologically rich languages are split into
many fragments — a phenomenon documented by \citet{rust2021howgood}.
LiB's MDL objective could in principle be applied to multilingual training data,
where it might discover a more equitable vocabulary that better represents
the distributional structure of each language.
We leave multilingual vocabulary learning to future work.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The current evaluation uses Wikipedia as the training corpus for all languages,
which may underrepresent colloquial registers and introduce topical biases.
The $n$-gram BPC metric provides a simple but limited view of tokenization quality;
downstream task performance in morphologically rich languages would provide a
more direct assessment.
Chinese preprocessing (no word segmentation) is an approximation; character-level
input may not be comparable to space-delimited input for the other languages.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% ============================================================

\paragraph{Subword tokenization.}
BPE \citep{sennrich-etal-2016-neural}, WordPiece \citep{schuster1999japanese},
and Unigram LM \citep{kudo-2018-subword} are the dominant subword tokenization
methods; SentencePiece \citep{kudo-richardson-2018-sentencepiece} provides
language-agnostic implementations of both.
\citet{bostrom2020bpe} show that BPE's greedy merge order recovers
morphologically less coherent units than Unigram LM, with measurable downstream
effects.
\citet{rust2021howgood} demonstrate that these methods systematically
under-represent morphologically rich languages in multilingual models;
\citet{toraman2023impact} confirm this for Turkish specifically, where a
morphology-aware tokenizer outperforms BPE on six downstream tasks.
\citet{mielke2021words} survey the full trajectory from word-level to
character-level to subword models and conclude that no single tokenization
approach is optimal across all languages.

\paragraph{Tokenization fairness and the cost of English bias.}
\citet{petrov2023unfairness} show that the same text translated into different
languages can differ in token count by up to 15$\times$ under standard
multilingual tokenizers.
\citet{ahia2023languages} document the economic consequence: speakers of
morphologically rich and non-Latin-script languages pay more API tokens per
task while receiving worse results.
\citet{gutierrez-vasques2023looking} analyse BPE compression across 47
languages and show that merge patterns cluster by typological distance from
English, with analytic languages compressed most efficiently.
\citet{zouhar2023tokenization} frame tokenization as an information channel and
show that Rényi entropy of a tokenization predicts downstream quality ($r=0.82$
with BLEU), providing an information-theoretic basis for MDL-based tokenizers.

\paragraph{MDL and tokenization.}
The connection between tokenization and MDL was noted by
\citet{brent1999efficient} for word segmentation.
\citet{yang2020less} formalised the LiB objective on small corpora;
\citet{yang2024rethinking} subsequently argued for an integrated sub- and
supra-word vocabulary grounded in the Principle of Least Effort.
\citet{yang2022eyefixations} showed that LiB segmentations predict
human eye fixations during reading better than word-level segmentations,
providing psycholinguistic grounding for the units LiB discovers.
The present work extends LiB to a production-grade multilingual setting.

\paragraph{Supra-word tokens.}
Multi-word expressions have a long history in NLP
\citep{sag2002multiword,constant2017multiword}.
\citet{gee2023multiword} enrich a pretrained tokenizer's vocabulary with
frequent MWEs, achieving $\sim$50\% sequence compression with negligible
performance loss.
Most closely related to LiB is \emph{SuperBPE} \citep{liu2025superbpe}, which
extends BPE merges across whitespace to produce multi-word ``superword'' tokens,
gaining up to $+4\%$ on average across 30 downstream tasks with 27\% less
inference compute.
LiB differs from SuperBPE in its motivation and mechanism: rather than
extending a greedy frequency criterion, LiB uses the MDL objective to
discover sub- and supra-word units jointly from first principles, with a
principled stopping criterion tied to information gain rather than a fixed
vocabulary budget.
The psycholinguistic motivation for treating multi-word units as lexical entries
is supported by \citet{wray2002formulaic}, \citet{siyanova2011collocation},
\citet{christiansen2017more}, and \citet{arnon2021starting}, who show that
multiword chunks are primary units of language acquisition and real-time
processing, not post-hoc constructions from words.

\paragraph{English bias in NLP and cognitive science.}
\citet{blasi2022overreliance} provide the broadest recent review of English bias
in cognitive science.
In NLP, English dominance in benchmarks, pre-training data, and model development
has been documented across tasks \citep{joshi2020state,bender2021stochastic}.
Tokenization is a relatively underexplored dimension of this bias; the present
work provides the first systematic MDL-based analysis across seven typologically
diverse language families.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ============================================================

We have presented LiB, a tokenizer grounded in the Minimum Description Length
principle, and argued that the English bias documented by
\citet{blasi2022overreliance} in cognitive science extends to tokenization.
The design assumptions of BPE, WordPiece, and SentencePiece — space-based
pre-tokenization, greedy character-pair merging, vocabulary sizes calibrated
on English — are ill-suited to the majority of the world's languages.
LiB's language-agnostic MDL objective adapts to each language's distributional
structure, discovering supra-word tokens for analytic languages and sub-word
tokens for morphologically rich languages.

Our multilingual evaluation across seven languages spanning analytic, fusional,
agglutinative, templatic, and logographic types reveals that LiB degrades most
gracefully on agglutinative languages: the gap between LiB and the best baseline
is only 13\% for Finnish and 15\% for Turkish, compared to 22--23\% for English,
German, and Spanish.
On Turkish, LiB's MDL criterion naturally saturates the vocabulary at 29k entries
rather than filling an arbitrary 50k budget, providing a principled, language-sensitive
stopping criterion absent from all baselines.
The supra-word token ratio — a new typological diagnostic introduced here — varies
systematically across language types (2\% for Chinese, 65--69\% for agglutinative,
76--81\% for analytic and templatic languages), providing a data-driven proxy
for the analytic-to-synthetic typological continuum.
LiB performs worst on Chinese (+43\% gap), where the absence of word-boundary
spaces prevents supra-word token formation and requires dedicated future treatment.

We release LiB as a HuggingFace-compatible Rust implementation and call for
its application to multilingual vocabulary learning, where the MDL objective
may yield more equitable representations across the world's languages.
