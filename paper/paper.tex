\begin{abstract}
Almost every modern tokenizer treats whitespace as a hard floor: no token may
span a word boundary, however frequent the collocation.
LiB (Less is Better) crosses that floor from first principles, learning
sub-word and supra-word units jointly under a single Minimum Description
Length (MDL) objective.
We present a production HuggingFace-compatible implementation of LiB and show
that the component that makes the MDL objective work in practice is
\emph{forgetting}: a cognitively grounded memorise-and-forget loop combining
an active route (ordinal reward re-ranking) and a passive route (probation
and decay of unused units).
With both routes operating, the vocabulary size is no longer a hyperparameter
but an emergent equilibrium: across seven typologically diverse languages,
LiB settles at 13.6k--28.4k units under a 50k cap.
Ablating passive forgetting inflates the lexicon by 38--88\% and worsens
description length by 2--10\% in every language, while leaving trigram
bits-per-character essentially unchanged — the retained units are dead weight
that a smoothed $n$-gram model never notices but MDL prices in full.
Budget-matched controls show that LiB's favourable description length stems
from its freedom to choose its own vocabulary size; on predictive metrics,
which are size-robust, LiB outperforms size-matched BPE on trigram
bits-per-character in six of seven languages and matches BPE on a
tokenization-fair neural bits-per-byte evaluation in all seven, while
outperforming SuperBPE, the leading frequency-based supra-word tokenizer, in
five.
Supra-word tokens help LiB on every metric in every language, and the
fraction of them LiB discovers doubles as a typological diagnostic, tracking
script and word-internal morphological complexity.
We release the Rust implementation, trained tokenizers, and all evaluation
code.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================

Almost every modern tokenizer refuses to place a token above the word.
Byte-Pair Encoding \citep[BPE;][]{sennrich-etal-2016-neural}, WordPiece
\citep{schuster1999japanese,wu2016googles}, and Unigram
\citep{kudo-2018-subword,kudo-richardson-2018-sentencepiece} all pre-tokenize
on whitespace and then learn units strictly \emph{within} the resulting
word-like chunks.
Whitespace is a hard floor: no matter how often \emph{of the} or
\emph{New York} recurs, these methods will never store it as a single token.
The convention is so universal that it is rarely stated as a design choice at
all — yet it is one, and an under-motivated one.
It was inherited from pipelines built for English, a language whose short,
mostly monomorphemic, space-delimited words make the word a convenient atom
\citep{blasi2022overreliance}; for the majority of the world's languages,
whose compressible structure sits below the word (agglutination, compounding,
templatic morphology) or above it (collocation), the floor is in the wrong
place.

Recent work has begun to question the floor.
SuperBPE \citep{liu2025superbpe} lets BPE merges cross whitespace in a second
training stage and reports downstream gains with substantially less inference
compute.
From a different tradition, the LiB tokenizer
\citep{yang2020less,yang2024rethinking} derives sub- and supra-word units
\emph{jointly} from a single Minimum Description Length (MDL) objective
\citep{rissanen1978modeling}, and \citet{yang2022eyefixations} show that its
segmentations predict human eye fixations during reading better than
word-based ones — behavioural evidence that multi-word units are
psycholinguistically real \citep{wray2002formulaic,arnon2021starting}.

This paper asks what it takes to make such an MDL tokenizer \emph{work} — at
production scale, across typologically diverse languages, and under honest
evaluation.
Our answer, in one word, is \emph{forgetting}.
An MDL lexicon must shed units as readily as it admits them, or it silts up
with candidates that never repay their storage cost.
Following \citet{yang2020less}, our implementation combines two forgetting
routes that mirror the active and passive forgetting observed in human
memory: an \emph{active} route that demotes — and eventually deletes — units
that lose head-to-head segmentation comparisons, and a \emph{passive} route
that places rarely used units on probation and lets them expire.
With both routes operating, the vocabulary size ceases to be a hyperparameter:
admission and forgetting reach a language-specific equilibrium well below the
nominal budget, and the units that survive earn their keep.

We evaluate on seven typologically diverse languages with a battery of
metrics — description length (DL), Kneser--Ney $n$-gram bits-per-character
(BPC), a tokenization-fair neural bits-per-byte (BPB) evaluation, sequence
fertility, and encoding throughput — and with controls that keep the
comparison honest, including a passive-forgetting ablation and
\emph{budget-matched} baselines trained at LiB's self-chosen vocabulary
sizes.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item We give an explicit algorithmic account of LiB's two-route
        memorise-and-forget training loop and show that forgetting is
        load-bearing: ablating the passive route inflates the lexicon by
        38--88\% and worsens DL by 2--10\% in all seven languages, while
        trigram BPC is unaffected — the ablated lexicon carries dead weight
        that only a description-length lens reveals.
  \item We show that the vocabulary size self-regulates (13.6k--28.4k under a
        50k cap, tracking the language), and we give an honest account of
        what that buys: budget-matched baselines reveal that LiB's DL
        advantage over standard 50k-vocabulary baselines is a
        vocabulary-size effect, while LiB's advantage on predictive,
        size-robust metrics survives the control — it beats size-matched BPE
        on trigram BPC in six of seven languages.
  \item We provide a tokenization-fair neural evaluation: under
        bits-per-byte with a small Transformer LM, LiB is at parity with BPE
        in all seven languages (winning outright on Turkish and Mandarin
        Chinese) and ahead of SuperBPE in five, while supra-word tokens help
        LiB on every metric in every language.
  \item We introduce the supra-word ratio of the induced vocabulary as a
        data-driven typological diagnostic, tracking script and
        word-internal morphological complexity.
  \item We release LiB as a production HuggingFace-compatible Rust
        implementation with trained tokenizers and all evaluation code.
\end{itemize}

\section{The LiB Tokenizer}
\label{sec:lib}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Objective}

The LiB objective \citep{yang2020less} is to find a token vocabulary $V$ and a
segmentation function $f$ such that the Description Length of the corpus is
minimised:

\begin{align}
  \mathrm{DL}(V, C) &= \mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{lex}(V) + \mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{corp}(C, V) \\
  \mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{lex}(V) &= -\sum_{s} \mathrm{Freq}(s) \log_2 P(s) \\
  \mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{corp}(C, V) &= -\sum_{u \in f(C)} \mathrm{Freq}(u) \log_2 P(u)
\end{align}

where $s$ ranges over the atomic symbols (characters) that make up the
vocabulary and $u$ ranges over the token types in the encoded corpus $C$.
Minimising the sum encourages both a compact, regular vocabulary
(small $\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{lex}$) and a highly compressive encoding
(small $\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{corp}$).

\subsection{Candidates Without a Whitespace Floor}

Training begins with a seed vocabulary of all observed characters.
Candidate tokens are concatenations of adjacent chunks in the current
segmentation of the training text, and crucially they span whatever
boundaries the text presents — there is no prior assumption about word or
morpheme boundaries:
\begin{itemize}
  \item In English, beneficial candidates often span spaces, yielding
        \emph{supra-word} tokens such as \texttt{"\_of\_the"} or
        \texttt{"\_New\_York"}.
  \item In German, candidates may remain within long compounds, yielding
        tokens corresponding to roots or affixes.
  \item In Finnish or Turkish, candidates may systematically recover
        morpheme-length units.
  \item In Mandarin Chinese, written in an unspaced logographic script,
        candidates operate over character sequences and discover frequent
        multi-character words.
\end{itemize}
Whether a candidate \emph{survives} is decided not at admission but by the
memorise-and-forget dynamics described next.

\input{forgetting_section}

\subsection{Space Convention}

Following the Metaspace convention used by BPE and SentencePiece, a leading
space is \emph{prepended} to each token to mark word-initial position
(e.g.\ \texttt{"\_cat"}).
For supra-word tokens, the space is infixed to mark internal word boundaries
(e.g.\ \texttt{"\_the\_cat"}).
This convention ensures lossless reconstruction of the original text and is
consistent with standard tokenizer behaviour.
For languages written without spaces (Chinese), text is fed without any space
insertion; LiB then operates directly on character sequences.

\subsection{Implementation}

We implement LiB as a custom \texttt{Model} in the HuggingFace
\texttt{tokenizers} Rust
library.\footnote{Fork: \url{https://github.com/antalvdb/tokenizers}, branch
\texttt{lib-model}.}
A Python wrapper (\texttt{LiBTokenizerFast}) exposes the HuggingFace
\texttt{PreTrainedTokenizerFast} interface.
The pre-trained English tokenizer is available at
\url{https://huggingface.co/antalvdb/lib-tokenizer}.

\subsection{Data}

For each language we sample 100,000 sentences for training and 10,000
sentences for evaluation from Wikipedia via the HuggingFace \texttt{datasets}
library \citep{lhoest2021datasets}.
Wikipedia provides topically consistent, high-quality text across all seven
languages with broad genre coverage.
Chinese text is used without word-boundary segmentation, allowing LiB and the
baselines to operate directly on character sequences.

\subsection{Tokenizers}

We compare the following tokenizers, all with a vocabulary budget of 50k:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{BPE}: HuggingFace \texttt{tokenizers} with Metaspace
        pre-tokenizer.
  \item \textbf{WordPiece}: HuggingFace \texttt{tokenizers} with whitespace
        pre-tokenizer.
  \item \textbf{SP-Unigram}: SentencePiece Unigram
        (\texttt{character\_coverage=0.9995}).
  \item \textbf{SuperBPE}: BPE with a second, cross-whitespace merge stage
        \citep{liu2025superbpe}, the leading frequency-based supra-word
        tokenizer.
  \item \textbf{LiB}: our implementation, evaluated with and without
        supra-word lookup, plus the ablations and controls below.
  \item \textbf{Symbol}: character baseline.
\end{itemize}

All tokenizers are trained on the per-language training set.
SentencePiece handles Chinese naturally through its byte-level fallback; BPE
and WordPiece are given character-split input for Chinese.

\paragraph{LiB configuration.}
Hyperparameters were selected by random search on held-out validation BPC for
English, Finnish, Turkish, and Chinese (Section~\ref{sec:hparam}).
The space-delimited languages share a consensus configuration
($\alpha{=}0.25$, $\Delta{=}0.4$, $\omega{=}0.005$, $\lambda{=}50$,
$d{=}100$, $\texttt{max\_len}{=}16$, 5{,}000 document-epochs); Chinese uses
its own searched configuration ($\omega{=}0.01$, $\lambda{=}10$, $d{=}50$,
$\texttt{max\_len}{=}20$).

\paragraph{Controls.}
Three additional LiB variants isolate the mechanisms at work:
\emph{LiB (no forget)} ablates passive forgetting ($\omega{=}0$, everything
else unchanged); \emph{LiB (cap)} and \emph{LiB (cap2)} raise the admission
rate ($\alpha{=}0.5$; cap2 additionally weakens decay to $\omega{=}0.001$) so
that the English lexicon approaches (46.7k) and pins (50.0k) the budget,
giving a budget ladder from the self-regulated size to the cap.
Finally, \emph{budget-matched baselines} retrain BPE, WordPiece, and
SP-Unigram at each language's emergent LiB vocabulary size, separating
vocabulary-size effects from tokenizer-quality effects.

\subsection{Metrics}

\paragraph{Description Length.}
$\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{lex}$, $\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{corp}$, and
$\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{total}$ in kilobits, following \citet{yang2020less}.
DL is LiB's own training objective; we therefore treat it as a diagnostic of
whether the mechanism achieves what it optimises, and pair it with the
independent metrics below.

\paragraph{Bits per Character (BPC).}
Kneser--Ney interpolated bigram and trigram language models
\citep{kneser-ney-1995}, trained with KenLM \citep{heafield2011kenlm} on the
tokenised training corpus and evaluated on the tokenised test corpus.
BPC = total surprisal / total characters in raw test text; the
character normalisation makes scores comparable across tokenizations.

\paragraph{Neural bits per byte (BPB).}
$n$-gram estimators penalise rare token types through data sparsity, which
systematically disadvantages any tokenizer with a long tail of supra-word
types.
As a tokenization-fair complement we train one small Transformer LM per
tokenizer (4 layers, 4 heads, $d{=}256$, context 256; identical architecture,
training schedule, and data for every tokenizer) and report cross-entropy
over the test stream normalised by its raw UTF-8 byte count.

\paragraph{Fertility.}
Tokens per byte on the test set, the determinant of sequence length and hence
inference cost \citep{liu2025superbpe}.

\paragraph{Supra-word token ratio.}
The proportion of vocabulary entries containing an \emph{internal} space
(a leading Metaspace marker does not count), a proxy for the degree to which
LiB invests its vocabulary above rather than below the word.

\paragraph{Encoding throughput.}
Characters per second, measured on an otherwise idle machine over 3
repetitions on 1,000 test lines after a warm-up pass.

Table~\ref{tab:dl_en} contains the central mechanism result.
With both forgetting routes active, LiB self-regulates to 28{,}418 units —
57\% of its budget — and lands second among the 50k-budget tokenizers,
within 1.4\% of WordPiece (7{,}924 vs.\ 7{,}814\,kb) and ahead of BPE and
SP-Unigram outright.
Ablating passive forgetting ($\omega{=}0$) inflates the lexicon to 41{,}682
units and DL to 8{,}373\,kb: the extra 13k units buy a modest 98\,kb of
corpus encoding but cost 548\,kb of lexicon — dead weight that MDL prices in
full.
The cap ladder shows the same monotonically: forcing the lexicon toward the
budget (46.7k, 50.0k) keeps improving $\mathrm{DL}_\mathrm{corp}$ and even
pushes the average token past BPE's length (5.16 vs.\ 5.01 characters), yet
total DL only degrades.
The equilibrium the forgetting dynamics find is, for LiB, the DL-optimal
operating point.

\subsection{The Budget-Matched Control}
\label{sec:budgetmatched}

The bottom block of Table~\ref{tab:dl_en} keeps this result honest.
Baselines retrained \emph{at LiB's emergent size} all improve on their 50k
versions, and all overtake LiB (WordPiece @28.4k: 7{,}405\,kb).
LiB's DL advantage over standard baselines is therefore a
\emph{vocabulary-size effect}: at these corpus sizes, description length
rewards smaller vocabularies for every tokenizer, and only LiB is free to
choose its size.
What the mechanism genuinely contributes is (i) finding a good size
autonomously, with no vocabulary-size hyperparameter to tune — the standard
budgets of 32k--50k are themselves an English-derived convention — and
(ii) as the ablation shows, the finding is done by forgetting.
We return to what survives on size-robust metrics below.

Ablating passive forgetting inflates every lexicon (by 38--88\%), most

The supra-word ratio doubles as a typological diagnostic, and it patterns on
two axes.
Script is decisive: written without spaces, Chinese has essentially no
supra-word tokens ($0.7\%$, which inspection shows to be mixed-script noise
rather than genuine multi-word units) — the supra-word mechanism is defined
over spaces and is simply inert without them.

morphological complexity \emph{inversely}: the agglutinative and compounding
languages, whose compressible redundancy sits inside the word, spend the
least budget above it (Finnish 14.7\%, German 17.5\%, Turkish 19.1\%), while
analytic, Romance, and templatic languages spend the most (English 25.7\%,
Spanish 29.6\%, Arabic 30.5\%).
LiB finds the redundancy where each language keeps it, with the same
objective and no language-specific rules.

presence of supra-word tokens that costs predictive quality, but how they
are selected and, crucially, whether the ones that stop paying are removed.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity}
\label{sec:hparam}

We ran random search (30--40 trials per language) over all five training
hyperparameters plus document size on English, Finnish, Turkish, and Chinese,
scoring held-out validation 3-gram BPC at the 50k cap.
The three space-delimited languages agree on a consensus configuration
($d{=}100$, $\texttt{max\_len}{=}16$, $\lambda{=}50$, $\omega{=}0.005$,
$\Delta{=}0.4$), which we adopt for all space-delimited languages.
The forgetting ratio $\omega$ is the dominant knob, governing the
admission--forgetting equilibrium (Section~\ref{sec:forgetting-results});
$\alpha$ trades vocabulary size against a marginal BPC gain (the
BPC-best $\alpha{=}0.5$ pins English at the cap for $\approx$0.01 bits over
the self-regulating $\alpha{=}0.25$, which we prefer for its emergent size
and faster training).
Remaining hyperparameters affect validation BPC by under 0.03 bits across
the searched ranges.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% ============================================================

\paragraph{What forgetting buys.}
The ablation isolates a clean causal story: with passive forgetting removed
and everything else held fixed, the lexicon inflates by 38--88\%, description
length worsens by 2--10\% in every language, and trigram BPC does not move.
The dissociation is the interesting part.
Prediction-based metrics are insensitive to dead weight — a unit that rarely
fires costs a smoothed LM almost nothing — so a vocabulary can look healthy
under BPC while carrying thousands of unearning types.
The MDL lens, and only the MDL lens, prices that storage; and the
memorise-and-forget dynamics, with their two routes mirroring active and
passive forgetting in human memory, are what keep the books balanced.
The $\omega$-sweep (Table~\ref{tab:omega}) shows the same mechanism from a
different angle: as forgetting strengthens, the never-used fraction of the
lexicon falls monotonically, and past a threshold the vocabulary stops
filling its cap and self-regulates.

\paragraph{An honest account of the DL result.}
Against the standard fixed-budget baselines, LiB attains the best description
length in five of seven languages; against baselines granted LiB's own
vocabulary size, it loses in all seven.
Both facts matter.
The first says the self-regulation is doing real work: the 32k--50k budget
convention — itself calibrated on English — leaves description length on the
table in every language, most dramatically the agglutinative ones (Finnish
$-19.5\%$).
The second says LiB's per-budget encoding is not better than the baselines',
and we do not claim it is.
The defensible claim is architectural: LiB is the only system in the
comparison for which vocabulary size is a \emph{result} — discovered per
language by the admission--forgetting equilibrium — rather than a
hyperparameter, and the discovered sizes are good ones.
For predictive quality the story is stronger: the BPC advantage over BPE
survives size-matching in six of seven languages, and neural BPB parity holds
at LiB's emergent sizes against baselines using their full budget.

\paragraph{Supra-word tokens vindicated — selection and retention matter.}
In the fixed implementation, supra-word tokens help LiB on every metric in
every spaced language: DL, 2-gram and 3-gram BPC, neural BPB, fertility, and
even throughput.
This contrasts sharply with SuperBPE, whose frequency-selected, never-forgotten
supra-words win on fertility but lose BPC to plain BPE in all seven
languages.
Together the two systems suggest that the supra-word level itself is not the
problem — how units are admitted, and above all whether unearning units are
\emph{removed}, determines whether crossing the whitespace floor pays.
The psycholinguistic evidence that LiB's units are cognitively real
\citep{yang2022eyefixations} gains a computational counterpart here: a
lexicon curated by forgetting behaves better than one that only grows.

\paragraph{The supra-word ratio as typology.}
The corrected supra-word ratios pattern cleanly on two axes: script first —
unspaced logographic Chinese has effectively no supra-word tokens, because
the mechanism is defined over spaces — and, among spaced languages,
word-internal morphological complexity second, with agglutinative and
compounding languages (Finnish 14.7\%, German 17.5\%, Turkish 19.1\%)
investing least above the word and analytic, Romance, and templatic
languages most (English 25.7\%, Spanish 29.6\%, Arabic 30.5\%).
The ratio operationalises where a language keeps its compressible
redundancy, discovered by a single objective with no language-specific
machinery.
Arabic's position at the top is no longer anomalous at these levels: Modern
Standard Arabic's frequent fixed formulae plausibly supply genuine
multi-word units, and at 30\% the ratio is comparable to Spanish rather
than the outlier it would be at the top of a 65--81\% range.

\paragraph{The label ``supra-word'' encodes an English bias.}
It is supra- relative to the English word, an unusually short and
monomorphemic unit by cross-linguistic standards.
What LiB discovers at the equivalent compression point in Finnish is
morpheme-length — sub-word in English terms, but linguistically fundamental
in Finnish.
The MDL objective makes no such distinction, which is precisely its value
for equitable multilingual tokenization
\citep{petrov2023unfairness,ahia2023languages}.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
DL is LiB's own training objective; we therefore lean on the independent
metrics (BPC, BPB, fertility) for cross-tokenizer claims, and on the
ablation — not the baseline comparison — for the mechanism claim.
Our neural evaluation uses a small LM; whether the 4--8\% BPB gap to the
subword leaders persists, shrinks, or reverses at scale, and how these
differences translate to downstream task quality, remains open — SuperBPE's
downstream gains \citep{liu2025superbpe} suggest intrinsic metrics
undersell supra-word tokenizers.
Wikipedia may underrepresent colloquial registers; Chinese preprocessing
(no word segmentation) makes its results a script-level, not
morphology-level, comparison; and the cap-filling variants change the
admission rate alongside decay, so the budget ladder is indicative rather
than a single-knob sweep above 46.7k.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% ============================================================

\paragraph{Subword tokenization.}
BPE \citep{sennrich-etal-2016-neural}, WordPiece \citep{schuster1999japanese},
and Unigram LM \citep{kudo-2018-subword} are the dominant subword tokenization
methods; SentencePiece \citep{kudo-richardson-2018-sentencepiece} provides
language-agnostic implementations of both.
\citet{bostrom2020bpe} show that BPE's greedy merge order recovers
morphologically less coherent units than Unigram LM, with measurable downstream
effects.
\citet{rust2021howgood} demonstrate that these methods systematically
under-represent morphologically rich languages in multilingual models;
\citet{toraman2023impact} confirm this for Turkish specifically, where a
morphology-aware tokenizer outperforms BPE on six downstream tasks.
\citet{mielke2021words} survey the full trajectory from word-level to
character-level to subword models and conclude that no single tokenization
approach is optimal across all languages.

\paragraph{Tokenization fairness and the cost of English bias.}
\citet{blasi2022overreliance} document cognitive science's over-reliance on
English; the same critique applies to tokenization's design defaults, from
whitespace pre-tokenization to the 32k--50k budget convention.
\citet{petrov2023unfairness} show that the same text translated into different
languages can differ in token count by up to 15$\times$ under standard
multilingual tokenizers.
\citet{ahia2023languages} document the economic consequence: speakers of
morphologically rich and non-Latin-script languages pay more API tokens per
task while receiving worse results.
\citet{gutierrez-vasques2023looking} analyse BPE compression across 47
languages and show that merge patterns cluster by typological distance from
English.
\citet{zouhar2023tokenization} frame tokenization as an information channel and
show that Rényi entropy of a tokenization predicts downstream quality,
providing an information-theoretic basis for MDL-based tokenizers.

\paragraph{MDL, segmentation, and forgetting.}
The connection between segmentation and MDL goes back to
\citet{brent1999efficient}.
\citet{yang2020less} formalised the LiB objective and built forgetting into
the model as a first-class mechanism; \citet{yang2024rethinking} argued for
an integrated sub- and supra-word vocabulary grounded in the Principle of
Least Effort, and \citet{yang2022eyefixations} showed that LiB segmentations
predict human eye fixations during reading.
The present work contributes the production-scale implementation, the
explicit active/passive algorithmic account, and the ablation evidence that
the forgetting mechanism — rather than the objective alone — is what makes
the approach competitive.

\paragraph{Supra-word tokens.}
Multi-word expressions have a long history in NLP
\citep{sag2002multiword,constant2017multiword}.
\citet{gee2023multiword} enrich a pretrained tokenizer's vocabulary with
frequent MWEs, achieving $\sim$50\% sequence compression with negligible
performance loss.
Most closely related is SuperBPE \citep{liu2025superbpe}, which extends BPE
merges across whitespace and gains up to $+4\%$ across 30 downstream tasks
with 27\% less inference compute.
LiB differs in mechanism and consequence: units are admitted by description
length rather than frequency, retained only while they earn their keep, and
the vocabulary size is discovered rather than imposed — yielding better
predictive quality than SuperBPE at equal budgets, at the cost of SuperBPE's
superior sequence compression.
The psycholinguistic case for multi-word lexical units is made by
\citet{wray2002formulaic}, \citet{siyanova2011collocation},
\citet{christiansen2017more}, and \citet{arnon2021starting}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ============================================================

We set out to build a production-grade tokenizer that learns sub- and
supra-word units jointly under a Minimum Description Length objective, and
found that the decisive component is not the objective but the memory
discipline that serves it.
A memorise-and-forget loop with two cognitively grounded routes — active
demotion of units that lose segmentation comparisons, passive expiry of units
that stop being used — turns the vocabulary size into a language-specific
emergent equilibrium and keeps the lexicon free of dead weight that
prediction-based metrics cannot even see.
Ablating passive forgetting inflates the lexicon by up to 88\% and worsens
description length in all seven languages while leaving trigram BPC
untouched: forgetting is load-bearing, and description length is the only
standard metric that notices.

Evaluated honestly — with budget-matched controls and a tokenization-fair
neural metric — LiB does not beat the best subword tokenizers at their own
game, and we do not claim it does.
It matches BPE's predictive quality with vocabularies half the size, beats
the frequency-based supra-word alternative on every predictive metric,
discovers where each language keeps its compressible redundancy, and sizes
its own vocabulary — removing a hyperparameter whose standard values are one
more English-derived convention.
Supra-word tokens, properly curated by forgetting, help on every metric in
every spaced language; the supra-word ratio itself emerges as a typological
diagnostic, near zero for unspaced logographic Chinese and inversely
tracking word-internal morphological complexity among spaced languages.
We release the Rust implementation, trained tokenizers, and evaluation
suite, and see two clear next steps: downstream evaluation at scale, where
SuperBPE's results suggest supra-word tokenizers are undersold by intrinsic
metrics, and multilingual joint vocabularies, where self-regulation may
yield more equitable allocations than any fixed budget.
